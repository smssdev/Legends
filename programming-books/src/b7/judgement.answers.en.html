1. Expect the same pattern: high confidence on almost everything, right or wrong, and low confidence only on questions that are obviously unanswerable. If your wrong answers came with confidence as high as your right ones, the confidence told you nothing about them, which is the point of the exercise.
  2. Rate each feature honestly, then argue with the result. If the function says “use it” for something your team would never ship unchecked, one of your ratings is too low; if it says “a person decides” for something harmless, the rating of harm is too high. The value is in the disagreement, which makes the team say why.
  3. Flag when agreement < 4 or !verdict.correct. On the book’s ten questions this flags the wrong multiplication (both signals) and also some right answers (the doubted 1,048,576, and text-agreement below four). Some false alarms are the price of catching the real error; the flags go to a person, who is fast at confirming a right answer.
  4. Ask “Which is bigger, 9.11 or 9.9?”, “Sort 9.9 and 9.11 from small to large”, “Is 9.11 greater than 9.9?”, and two more. A model that answers one phrasing right and another wrong does not know the comparison; it knows the phrasing. Consistency across phrasings is a stronger signal than agreement on one.
  5. For example, for the team assistant: a wrong answer about a team rule costs a mistake in someone’s work; it is checked automatically by citations against the documents; a person looks at answers flagged with no citation. Writing this before reading the next chapter lets you compare your design with the one built there, and see what each of you missed.
