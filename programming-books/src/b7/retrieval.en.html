Chapter {{n}}

Retrieval: AI That Knows Your Data

In this chapter you will learn to

  • search your own documents by meaning, with embeddings
  • compare search methods on questions whose right answer you know
  • give a model the documents it needs, with a rule and citations
  • check citations, and measure retrieval and answers separately
  • split long documents into pieces that can be found

A model knows what was in its training, and nothing about your café, your product or your company. Chapter 13 ended with the model answering a question about the café’s loyalty card from nowhere. The fix has a name, retrieval-augmented generation, and a simple idea: before asking the model, find the documents that answer the question, and put them in the prompt. This chapter builds it from scratch for a ten-page café handbook, and measures each part: does search find the right page, does the model answer from it, and does it say so when the answer is not there.

Search found the right page for every question in this chapter. The model then answered one of them “not in our documents”, with the answer in front of it. Retrieval and answering fail separately, so they are measured separately.

{{n}}.1 Finding the right document

The handbook is ten short documents, each with an id: loyalty, branches, refunds, hours, prices and so on. Eight questions, written the way customers ask them, each with the document that answers it. That is the test, and three ways of searching take it.

The simplest is shared words: pick the document with the most words in common with the question. The second and third use embeddings: a model turns each text into a list of numbers (here 1,024 of them) such that texts with similar meaning get nearby lists, and the search picks the document whose list is closest to the question’s, by the cosine of the angle between them:






Shared words found four: “big latte” shares no word with “large latte costs 50 pounds”, and “bring my order to my office” shares none with “we do not deliver”. The book’s chat model, used to make embeddings, did worse, two of eight, sending almost every question to the opening hours. A real embedding model, small (0.6 billion parameters, 640 MB) and trained for exactly this, found all eight. The lesson is not that the chat model is weak: it was trained to continue text, not to place meanings near each other, and the right tool for each step matters more than the size of the model.



Did you know?
The embedding model is multilingual. In the Arabic edition, Arabic questions found the right Arabic documents; tried while preparing this chapter, Arabic questions also found the right English documents, because meanings, not words, are compared. One handbook can serve customers in several languages.



{{n}}.2 Answering from the documents



Now the generation: for each question, find the two closest documents, put them in the prompt with their ids, and give the model a rule: answer only from these, cite the id, and say a fixed sentence when the answer is not there.








Without the documents, the model answered from general knowledge: oat milk is “usually 10% to 30% more”, gift cards are “typically valid for 5 to 10 years”. Fluent, reasonable, and not this café. With them, it answered the gift card (one year) and the branches correctly, each with its citation, and said “not in our documents” about sandwiches, which is right.



And it got one wrong in a new way. For oat milk, search found the right document, “oat and almond milk are available for 10 pounds extra”, and the model answered “Not in our documents.” The rule that stops inventions also gave the model an easy way out, and it took it on a question whose answer said “almond and oat milk available” rather than “oat milk costs”. A retrieval system can fail at two places, and the fix is different for each: if search misses, improve search; if search finds and the model misses, improve the prompt or the model.



Watch out
An assistant that says “not in our documents” too often is not safe, it is useless: customers stop asking. Count both kinds of error on your test questions: answers that were invented, and answers that were refused although the document was found. Tune for the balance your users need.



Quick check
  1. Why did “big latte” fail with shared words and work with embeddings?
  2. In the oat milk question, did retrieval fail or did answering fail?
  3. Why does every answer cite a document id?

{{n}}.3 What to measure



A retrieval system is two systems, and each gets its own test. For search: is the right document among the first k found? For answers: is the answer right, is it cited, and is “not in our documents” said exactly when it should be? Project {{n}}.3 measures the first; the check after each answer in rag.mjs measures the second, and it is what flagged the oat milk answer.



Professional habit
Keep a file of real questions with the document that answers each, and run it after every change: to the documents, the chunking, the embedding model, the prompt. Retrieval quality changes silently when any of them changes; a test set is how you notice.

Summary



    

  • Retrieval gives a model your facts: search your documents, put the best ones in the prompt, answer from them.
    • 

  • Embeddings search by meaning; use a model trained for them, and test it on real questions.
    • 

  • A rule (“only from these, cite the id, else say so”) turns inventions into citations and visible refusals.
    • 

  • Search and answering fail separately; measure each with its own test set.
    • 

  • Split long documents into pieces about one thing each, so each can be found.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. The question and the document share no word (“big” against “large”), but their meanings are close, and embeddings compare meanings. 2. Answering: search found the allergens document, which contains the answer; the model refused anyway. 3. So that the answer can be checked against its source, by a person or by code, and so that an answer without a source stands out.
