Chapter {{n}}

From Idea to Specification and Tasks

In this chapter you will learn to

  • use a model to find the questions an idea leaves open, and see which it misses
  • turn answers into a specification with testable acceptance criteria
  • check a specification mechanically: vague words, criteria in Given/When/Then form, and contradictions
  • break a specification into tasks, and check the plan as a graph
  • treat a model’s estimates as a range, not a number

Most software that goes wrong goes wrong before the first line of code: the idea was vague, nobody asked the awkward questions, and the team built something slightly different from what was meant. Assistants are good at the early work: they ask questions, draft specifications and break them into tasks, in minutes. This chapter does that for a small, real feature, and measures each step: which questions were asked, which criteria can be tested, whether the plan holds together.

The specification in this chapter reads well and contradicts the café’s answers in two places. A checker for the form of the criteria caught one kind of problem; only reading the rules against the answers caught the other.

{{n}}.1 Questions before a specification

The idea is one sentence: “a loyalty card in our café app: every 10th coffee is free.” Before anyone writes a specification, ask what must be decided:






Ten reasonable questions, and the patterns at the end score them against seven that experience says matter for a loyalty card. The model asked two: what counts as a coffee, and how to prevent abuse. It did not ask about refunds, expiry, what the free drink can be, the second branch, or customers who already bought coffee before the card existed. Each of those becomes a bug report or an angry customer if nobody decides. Instead it asked three variations of “how are purchases counted” and one about the business goal.



So use the model’s questions as a start, not a checklist. It knows what loyalty cards look like in general; you know your café, your systems and the ways your customers surprise you. Keep your own list of questions that matter, as the program does, and check every draft against it.



Tip
Ask the model for questions in several passes, each from one point of view: the customer, the cashier, the accountant, the person who handles refunds, an attacker. A single broad request gets the obvious questions; a narrow point of view gets the awkward ones.



{{n}}.2 A specification, checked



With the café’s answers (only paid hot drinks count, stamps never expire, a refund removes its stamp, the free drink is any hot drink up to the price of a large latte, both branches share the card), ask for a specification with acceptance criteria as Given / When / Then: the situation, the action, the result. Then check it:








No vague words, six criteria, and five not in the form: they have a Given and a Then, and no When. “Given a user purchases a non-hot drink, Then the drink does not count” has no action to test; a tester has to guess what to do. That is the mechanical part, and the checker found it.



The checker cannot find the worse problems, which need a person reading the criteria against the answers:


    

  • “Then the system stamps the loyalty card if it is the 10th such drink.” Every paid hot drink gets a stamp; the 10th stamp earns the free drink. As written, a customer gets one stamp per ten drinks, and a free drink after a hundred.
    • 

  • “A hot drink priced above the price of a large latte does not count.” The café said the free drink is limited to the price of a large latte. The model moved the limit to the wrong rule: expensive drinks would stop earning stamps.
    • 

  • Nothing says what happens on the 10th stamp: how the free drink is offered, or that the count starts again.
    • 




Both errors come from the same place as Chapter 1’s inventions: the specification is the most likely continuation of “loyalty card rules”, and the café’s answers pulled it only part of the way. A specification is where a misunderstanding is cheapest to fix, so it is where to read most carefully.



Watch out
A model rewrites your requirements in its own words, and its own words drift toward the typical case. After it drafts a specification, put each rule next to the decision it came from, one by one. A rule that cannot be traced to a decision was invented; a decision with no rule was dropped.



Quick check
  1. What is missing from “Given a user has a loyalty card from one branch, Then the card is valid at both branches”?
  2. Which of the café’s answers did the specification apply to the wrong rule?
  3. Why did the model’s questions miss refunds and expiry?
The answers are at the end of the chapter, after the summary.



{{n}}.3 Tasks, checked as a graph



A plan is a list of tasks and the order they depend on. Ask for it as structured data, then check what can be checked: every dependency exists, there is no cycle, and there is an order in which everything can be done.








The graph is sound: no missing dependency, no cycle, and a valid order. The content inherits the specification’s errors: T3 checks drink eligibility against “price below large latte threshold”, the wrong rule from Section {{n}}.2, now with six hours of work attached. And it has gaps a team would notice: nothing for the free drink at checkout, nothing for the app screen that shows the stamps, nothing to migrate existing customers. A task list is a specification restated, and it is only as right as the specification.



Professional habit
Before a plan goes to the team, walk one customer through it: the first coffee, the tenth, the free one, a refund, a visit to the other branch. Every step of the walk should land in a task. The steps that land nowhere are the work the plan forgot.

Summary



    

  • Use the model to find open questions, then check them against your own list: it asks the typical ones.
    • 

  • Write acceptance criteria as Given / When / Then, and check the form with a program.
    • 

  • Read every rule of a drafted specification against the decision it came from; drift toward the typical case is the common error.
    • 

  • Check a task list as a graph, and walk a real case through it to find missing work.
    • 

  • Model estimates move from run to run; use them as a range for discussion, not a commitment.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. The When: an action to perform, such as “When they buy a hot drink at the other branch”. Without it, the tester has to invent the test. 2. The price limit: the café limited the free drink to the price of a large latte; the specification stopped expensive drinks from earning stamps. 3. Because the most likely questions about “every 10th coffee is free” are about counting; refunds and expiry come from experience with real customers, not from the sentence.
