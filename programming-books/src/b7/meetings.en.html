Chapter {{n}}

Meetings, Emails and Reports

In this chapter you will learn to

  • summarise a meeting into decisions and actions, and check the summary against what was said
  • draft emails and announcements, and check every number against its source
  • draft status reports, and check every “done” against the work itself
  • change the tone of a text without changing its facts
  • summarise text that is too long for one request, in parts

Much of a developer’s week is not code: meetings, emails, updates, reports. It is also where assistants save the most time, because the writing is routine and the readers are busy. And it is where their mistakes travel furthest: a wrong date in a summary becomes a missed deadline; a wrong price in an announcement reaches every customer. This chapter drafts all three kinds of text with the book’s model, and for each one asks the same question: where did this fact come from?

The meeting summary in this chapter has no decisions in it, though four were made, and dates with a year nobody said. The email changed the currency. The formal rewrite added a year. None of it is malicious; all of it is the model filling gaps with the typical case.

{{n}}.1 A meeting, summarised and checked

A short team meeting about the café: the loyalty card, a price change, new Friday hours. Four decisions are made, two proposals are turned down, and three people take on a task. A summary should keep all of that, and add nothing:








The actions are right: Hassan, Sara and Omar, with the right tasks. Two of them fail the check, for the same reason: “2023-12-04” and “2023-12-15”. The meeting said “Thursday the 4th of December”; the model turned it into a full date and chose a year, the wrong one. In a calendar, that is a deadline two years in the past.



And the decisions list is empty. The launch date, the price change, the Friday hours, both branches together: four decisions, and the most important part of any meeting summary, and the model returned none, though the schema asked for them. A summary is judged by what it leaves out as much as by what it says, and nobody notices an absence by reading. That is why the program compares the count with what the meeting really decided.



Tip
Tell the model to copy dates as they were said (“Thursday the 4th of December”), never to complete them. A date with a year or a weekday the speaker did not give is a guess, and guesses in summaries become commitments.



{{n}}.2 An email, with every number checked



An announcement to customers, from three facts. The check is simple and catches the worst mistakes: every number in the email must appear in the facts.








Every number has a source: 50, 45, the 1st, the 8th. The check passes, and the email is still wrong: “£50”. The facts said pounds, and this café is in Cairo; the model chose the pound sign it has seen most, the British one. Customers would read a price in sterling. No number changed, so no number check could see it. Read every unit, currency and name yourself; the check covers digits, not meaning.



Watch out
Anything sent to customers, partners or the whole company is read by people who will act on it, and cannot be recalled. Draft with a model; send only what a person has read against the facts, every number and every unit, with the facts beside them.



Quick check
  1. Why did the date check flag Hassan’s action, though the day and month were right?
  2. What did the number check miss in the email?
  3. Why is a missing decision harder to notice than a wrong one?
The answers are at the end of the chapter, after the summary.



{{n}}.3 A status report, checked against the work



A weekly report from the commit log and the task list. The danger in status reports is optimism: work described as done that is not. So the program sets the report beside the truth from the log:








The report agrees with the log on every task: four done, two remaining. Most of it is good, and a manager would read it in a minute. Two sentences go further than the evidence. T4 is “verified and implemented”, but its only commit is a test (test(loyalty): …); a test proves the behaviour was checked, not that new code was written. And “all core business logic is now in place” is a judgement nobody made: the free drink at checkout, still open, is core business logic too. A status report is where small overstatements add up to a project that is “nearly done” for months.



Professional habit
Link every “done” in a report to its evidence: a commit, a merged pull request, a deployed version. Ask the model to include the links; then a reader can check any claim in one click, and claims without a link stand out.

Summary



    

  • A model drafts meetings, emails and reports quickly; every fact in them needs a source.
    • 

  • Check summaries for what is missing as well as what is wrong: count decisions and actions against the source.
    • 

  • Check every number against its facts by code, and read units, currencies and names yourself.
    • 

  • Check every “done” in a report against the log, and watch for summaries that go further than the evidence.
    • 

  • Long text can be summarised in parts and merged, if the merge keeps everything the parts found.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. The model wrote “2023-12-04”: it added a year the meeting never said, and 2023 does not appear in the meeting. 2. The currency: “£50” turns Egyptian pounds into British ones, and no number changed. 3. A wrong decision is in front of you when you read; a missing one is not there to be seen, so only comparing with the source, or counting, reveals it.
