Chapter {{n}}

Judgement: When Not to Trust the AI

In this chapter you will learn to

  • see why a model’s confidence is not evidence
  • use agreement between samples, and self-checks, for what they are worth
  • send each kind of question to the tool that answers it reliably
  • decide how much to trust a model on a task, from its impact, reversibility and checkability
  • gather the lessons of the book into habits you can apply to any new tool

Every chapter of this book found the model wrong somewhere, and every chapter caught it with a check. That is not a story about a weak model; larger ones fail less often, in the same ways. It is a story about judgement: knowing which answers to trust, which to verify, and which tasks a model should not be given at all. This chapter measures the signals people use to decide (the model’s confidence, agreement between answers, the model checking itself) and finds out which of them deserve your trust.

The model in this chapter was 100% sure of a wrong multiplication. That one line is the chapter. The rest is how to work well anyway.

{{n}}.1 Confidence is not evidence

Ten questions with known answers: some arithmetic, some facts, a famous trap (9.11 or 9.9), a letter count, and two questions no model can answer, the café’s latte price and the winner of a World Cup that has not happened. For each, the model gives an answer and its confidence:






Nine right, and the two unknowable questions answered “unknown” with low confidence, which is good behaviour. And one wrong: 48213 × 7.35 is 354,365.55, and the model said 356,385.55, with 100% confidence. In the Arabic edition, a different wrong number, at 98%. Every right answer also came with 100%, so the number carries almost no information: a wrong answer and a right one look identical.



This is the central fact of working with models. They do not know what they do not know, except in the clear cases (“who won in 2034?”) that you would have caught yourself. Their confidence, in words or in numbers, is part of the generated text, and the most likely text after a confident question is a confident answer.



Watch out
Never use a model’s stated confidence to decide whether to check its answer. Decide by the task: what happens if the answer is wrong, and how easy it is to check. Section {{n}}.4 turns that into a rule.



{{n}}.2 Agreement between answers



A better signal: ask the same question several times with some randomness, and see whether the answers agree. A model that knows something tends to say it the same way; a model that is guessing guesses differently each time.








The wrong multiplication had the lowest agreement, one of five: five samples, five different numbers. That is a real warning, and it costs only extra calls. But read the other lines: “366” agreed three times of five and the letter count twice, both right, because the samples said the same thing in different words (“366 days”, “366”). Agreement must be measured on the extracted answer, not on the text, or right answers look shaky. In the Arabic edition, the letter count agreed three of five on 2, wrong: agreement is a signal, not a proof.



{{n}}.3 Asking the model to check itself








The self-check caught the wrong multiplication, in both editions. It also doubted a right answer (1,048,576), and in the Arabic edition it doubted 408, and confirmed a wrong letter count. A second look by the same model is a second sample, with the same blind spots, not an independent review. It is worth having when it is cheap, and its “wrong” is worth a human look; its “correct” proves little.

  1. What did the model’s confidence say about its one wrong answer?
  2. Why did “366” agree only three times of five, although every sample was right?

{{n}}.4 Deciding how far to trust



The signals help; the decision comes from the task. Three questions decide how much checking a task needs: how much harm a wrong answer does, whether it can be undone, and how easy it is to check. Project {{n}}.2 turns them into a small function; here is the rule in words:





The taskUse the model…Examples from this book



Low harm, easy to checkfreely, with a glancename ideas, first drafts, explanations of code you can read


Real harm, but checkableand verify before actingcode (tests), summaries (the source), numbers (code), citations (the documents)


Real harm, hard to undo or to checkonly to prepare; a person decidesrefunds, prices sent to customers, agents with write access, anything legal or medical


Exact answers that code can givenot at allarithmetic, counting, sorting, dates (Project {{n}}.1)






Professional habit
For every AI feature you build, write down three things: what a wrong answer costs, what checks it automatically, and who looks at what the checks cannot see. If you cannot fill in the second and third, the feature is not ready, however good the demo.



{{n}}.5 What the book measured



Gathered from the chapters, the pattern is consistent. The model wrote correct code and wrong tests (4, 7); explained code fluently and predicted its results three times in six (5); fixed a bug for the wrong reason (6); turned 20,000 piastres into dollars (5) and Egyptian pounds into sterling (10); invented a year in every date it reformatted (10); reported tasks done that had no commit (10); told customers about a discount an attacker wrote (15); and, given the right documents and the right checks, answered correctly and said “not in our documents” when that was the truth (14). The model was the same throughout. What changed the outcome was the work around it.

Summary



    

  • A model’s confidence is generated text, not evidence; it was 100% sure of a wrong answer.
    • 

  • Agreement between samples is a useful warning when measured on extracted answers; it is not proof.
    • 

  • A model checking itself shares its own blind spots; independent checks are code, sources and people.
    • 

  • Send exact questions to code, and decide the level of trust from the harm, reversibility and checkability of each task.
    • 

  • Across the book, the same model succeeded or failed depending on the work around it: the instructions, the facts, the tools and the checks.
    •
