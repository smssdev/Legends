Chapter {{n}}

Queues and Jobs

In this chapter you will learn to

  • decide which work belongs in a request and which belongs in a queued job
  • write jobs, dispatch them to a Redis queue, and run them with queue:work
  • retry failed jobs with growing pauses, and stop retrying when it is pointless
  • make jobs safe to run twice, and stop the same job from being queued twice
  • dispatch only after the database commits, and schedule work for fixed times
  • run and watch workers in production

When a customer places an order, the shop has more to do than save it: send a confirmation email, notify the warehouse, update a sales report, maybe resize the photos of a review. If the request does all of that, the customer waits for the slowest service in the chain, and when the mail provider has a bad minute, the order fails even though the order itself was fine. The answer is to do only the essential part in the request, write down the rest as jobs, and let separate processes do them a moment later. Laravel has queues built in; this chapter runs them on the Redis from Chapter {{n-3}}, for real.

Our first shop sent its emails inside the order request. One Friday evening the mail provider slowed to ten seconds per email, and our checkout took ten seconds too. Customers pressed “pay” again, and some were charged twice. Moving the emails into a queue took an afternoon; it would have saved us that whole weekend.

{{n}}.1 What belongs in a job

A request should do what the response depends on, and nothing more. Everything else is a candidate for a job:

  • Slow work: sending email and notifications, generating PDFs and reports, resizing images, calling slow partner APIs.
  • Unreliable work: anything that talks to another service, which will sometimes be down and needs retries.
  • Work at a later time: “remind the customer in 24 hours if the basket is still full”, “the daily sales report at 6 in the morning”.
  • Heavy work: imports and exports of thousands of rows, which would block a PHP worker for minutes.

A queue connects the two sides. The web request dispatches a job (a class and its data) onto the queue and answers at once. A worker, the long-running command php artisan queue:work, takes jobs from the queue and runs them. Redis keeps the queue, so jobs survive a restart of either side, and you can run as many workers as the work needs. Three settings in .env choose the backends: QUEUE_CONNECTION=redis, CACHE_STORE=redis and REDIS_CLIENT=predis (after composer require predis/predis, or install the faster phpredis extension instead).

{{n}}.2 A job and a worker

A job is a class with a handle method; php artisan make:job SendOrderConfirmation creates one. Implementing ShouldQueue is what sends it to the queue instead of running it at once:




    

  • The constructor’s properties are the job’s data, serialised into the queue. The job carries only the order id, and handle reads the order when it runs, so it always sees the current data.
    • 

  • handle can ask for services, such as the Mailer, and the container passes them in, exactly as in a controller.
    • 

  • $tries and $backoff decide how failures are retried; failed() runs once, after the last attempt.

{{n}}.3 Retries and failures



Retrying is what makes jobs reliable, and the job’s properties decide how:


    

  • $tries = 3 allows three attempts in total (or --tries on the worker for every job).
    • 

  • $backoff = [1, 2] waits 1 second before the second attempt and 2 before the third. Growing pauses give a struggling service time to recover, instead of hitting it again at once with every queued job. In real systems the waits are longer, such as [10, 60, 300].
    • 

  • Some errors will never go away: an invalid address, an order that no longer exists. $this->fail() ends the job at once, skipping the remaining attempts, as order 1004 shows. For errors that can be retried, simply throw.
    • 




A job that runs out of attempts is not lost. It is stored in the failed_jobs table with its exception, and its failed() method runs (here it writes to the log):






In production, failed jobs are a signal: count them and alert when they grow (Chapter {{n+3}}). After the cause is fixed, php artisan queue:retry puts them back on the queue (exercise 4); queue:forget and queue:flush remove them.



Watch out: a job can run twice
Queues promise that a job runs at least once, not exactly once. A worker can be killed after sending the email but before telling Redis the job is done; after retry_after seconds (in config/queue.php) the job is given to a worker again. Every job must be idempotent: running it twice has the same effect as running it once. Check before acting (“is the confirmation for order 1001 already marked as sent?”), give the mail provider an idempotency key, and make database changes with conditions, as in Chapter {{n-4}}. retry_after must also be longer than your slowest job, or a job still running is started a second time.



{{n}}.4 Jobs and the database



The order is saved in PostgreSQL and the job in Redis, and the two cannot share a transaction. Dispatching inside the transaction has two gaps: if it rolls back, the worker may send a confirmation for an order that does not exist; and the worker may run before the commit and not find the order. ->afterCommit() on the dispatch, or 'after_commit' => true for the whole connection in config/queue.php, holds the job back until the transaction commits, and drops it if the transaction rolls back.



One gap remains: if the process dies between the commit and the dispatch, the order exists and its confirmation is never sent. For emails that is usually acceptable and easy to repair with a scheduled check (“orders older than ten minutes without a sent confirmation”). When it is not, use the outbox from Chapter {{n-4}}: insert the message into an outbox table in the same transaction as the order, and let a scheduled command move outbox rows into the queue. Two more habits keep jobs and data in step:


    

  • Put ids in jobs, not data. The job above carries orderId and reads the email when it runs. With Eloquent models in the constructor, the SerializesModels behaviour of Queueable does the same: it stores the model’s id and loads it fresh in the worker.
    • 

  • Keep job data small and free of secrets: it is stored in Redis and in failed_jobs. Implement ShouldBeEncrypted for jobs that must carry personal data.
    •

{{n}}.5 Scheduled work



Some jobs are not caused by a request but by the clock. Laravel’s scheduler declares them in routes/console.php; here the sales report every morning at six, Cairo time:









The list shows the cron expression in the server’s time zone, UTC: 0 4 * * * is four in the morning UTC, six in Cairo. Always give a time zone, or the report moves by an hour twice a year. On the server, one cron entry runs php artisan schedule:run every minute (or a process runs php artisan schedule:work), and Laravel decides what is due. With several servers, add ->onOneServer(), which takes a lock in the shared cache so that the job is queued once, not once per server.



{{n}}.6 Running workers in production



Workers are long-running processes, and they need the same care as web servers:


    

  • A process manager. Supervisor or systemd (or the container platform of Chapter {{n+6}}) starts queue:work, restarts it if it crashes, and runs as many copies as you need.
    • 

  • Restart after every deploy. A worker loads the code once and keeps it in memory, so it keeps running the old code until restarted. php artisan queue:restart tells every worker to finish its current job and exit, and the process manager starts the new code. The same happens gracefully on SIGTERM.
    • 

  • Limits. --max-jobs and --max-time make a worker exit after a while, which keeps memory leaks from growing forever; $timeout on a job kills one that hangs.
    • 

  • Different queues for different work. ->onQueue('emails') on the dispatch, and queue:work --queue=emails,default, keep password-reset emails from waiting behind slow exports.
    • 

  • Visibility. php artisan queue:monitor redis:default --max=100 raises an event when a queue grows too long; Horizon, Laravel’s free dashboard for Redis queues, shows throughput, waiting times and failures, and manages the workers itself.
    • 




Professional habit: design every job to be retried
When you write a job, ask three questions before the code: What happens if it runs twice? What happens if it fails halfway? What happens if it runs an hour late? Write the answers as a comment above handle, and make the code match them. Jobs that survive these three questions survive production.

Summary



    

  • A request does what its response needs; slow, unreliable or later work becomes a job on a queue.
    • 

  • A job implements ShouldQueue; dispatching takes a millisecond, and queue:work runs jobs in separate processes.
    • 

  • $tries and $backoff make jobs reliable; $this->fail() stops pointless retries; failed jobs wait in failed_jobs for queue:retry.
    • 

  • Jobs run at least once: make them idempotent; ShouldBeUnique keeps one job per order.
    • 

  • Dispatch after the commit or through an outbox; put ids in jobs, not copies of data.
    • 

  • The scheduler runs timed jobs in a time zone; workers are supervised, restarted after deploys, and watched.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. The job implements ShouldBeUnique with uniqueId() returning order-1001; the first dispatch took a lock with that name in the cache, so the second was dropped while the first was still waiting. 2. A thrown exception counts as a failed attempt, and the job is retried after the backoff until $tries is used up; fail() marks the job as failed for good at once, runs failed() and stores it in failed_jobs, without more attempts. 3. The job is held back until the transaction commits; if it rolls back, the job is never queued, so no confirmation goes out for an order that was never saved.

Project {{n}}.3 · Challenge


An outbox relay


Guarantee that every committed order gets its confirmation job, even if the process dies right after the commit.


You will practise: the outbox pattern, lockForUpdate()->skipLocked(), the scheduler, and at-least-once delivery.


    

  1. In the order transaction, insert a row into outbox (type, payload, created) instead of dispatching.
    2. 

  2. Write a command, scheduled every minute with withoutOverlapping(), that takes up to 100 unsent rows with lockForUpdate()->skipLocked(), dispatches each, and marks them sent.
