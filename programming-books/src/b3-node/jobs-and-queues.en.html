Chapter {{n}}

Background Jobs and Queues

In this chapter you will learn to

  • decide which work belongs in a request and which belongs in a background job
  • queue jobs with BullMQ and Redis, and run them in a separate worker process
  • retry failed jobs with growing pauses, and stop retrying when it is pointless
  • make jobs safe to run twice, and stop the same job from being queued twice
  • schedule work for fixed times, and shut workers down without losing jobs
  • keep queues and the database consistent with each other

When a customer places an order, the shop has more to do than save it: send a confirmation email, notify the warehouse, update a sales report, maybe resize the photos of a review. If the request does all of that, the customer waits for the slowest service in the chain, and when the mail provider has a bad minute, the order fails even though the order itself was fine. The answer is to do only the essential part in the request, write down the rest as jobs, and let separate processes do them a moment later. This chapter builds that with BullMQ on the Redis from Chapter {{n-3}}, and runs every part of it for real.

Our first shop sent its emails inside the order request. One Friday evening the mail provider slowed to ten seconds per email, and our checkout took ten seconds too. Customers pressed “pay” again, and some were charged twice. Moving the emails into a queue took an afternoon; it would have saved us that whole weekend.

{{n}}.1 What belongs in a job

A request should do what the response depends on, and nothing more. Everything else is a candidate for a job:

  • Slow work: sending email and push notifications, generating PDFs and reports, resizing images, calling slow partner APIs.
  • Unreliable work: anything that talks to another service, which will sometimes be down and needs retries.
  • Work at a later time: “remind the customer in 24 hours if the basket is still full”, “the daily sales report at 6 in the morning”.
  • Heavy work: imports and exports of thousands of rows, which would block a web process for minutes.

A queue connects the two sides. The web process is the producer: it adds a job (a name and some data) to the queue and answers at once. A worker is a separate process that takes jobs from the queue and does them. Redis keeps the queue, so jobs survive a restart of either side, and you can run as many workers as the work needs.

{{n}}.3 Retries and failures



Retrying is what makes jobs reliable, and BullMQ’s options decide how:


    

  • attempts: 3 allows three tries in total.
    • 

  • backoff: exponential with delay: 1000 waits 1, 2, 4… seconds between tries. Growing pauses give a struggling service time to recover, instead of hitting it again at once with every queued job. (In real systems, add jitter, a little randomness, so that a thousand jobs that failed together do not all retry in the same second.)
    • 

  • Some errors will never go away: an invalid address, an order that no longer exists. Throwing BullMQ’s UnrecoverableError skips the remaining attempts, as order 1004 shows.
    • 




A job that runs out of attempts is not lost. It moves to the failed list with its reason, and stays there (for a week, by removeOnFail) until someone looks:









In production, failed jobs are a signal: count them on a dashboard and alert when they grow (Chapter {{n+3}}). After the cause is fixed, they can be retried (exercise 4). Some teams call this list a dead-letter queue.



Watch out: a job can run twice
Queues promise that a job runs at least once, not exactly once. A worker can crash after sending the email but before telling Redis the job is done; the job then runs again on another worker. Every job must be idempotent: running it twice has the same effect as running it once. Check before acting (“is the confirmation for order 1001 already marked as sent?”), give the mail provider an idempotency key such as order-1001-confirmation, and make database changes with conditions, as in Chapter {{n-4}}.



{{n}}.4 Jobs and the database



The order is saved in PostgreSQL and the job in Redis, and the two cannot share a transaction. Two orders of steps are possible, and both have a gap:


    

  • Queue, then commit: if the transaction then rolls back, the worker may already be sending a confirmation for an order that does not exist. Worse, the worker may run before the commit and not find the order.
    • 

  • Commit, then queue: if the process dies between the two, the order exists and its confirmation is never sent.
    • 




The second is usually acceptable for emails and easy to repair with a periodic check (“orders older than ten minutes without a sent confirmation”). When it is not, use the outbox from Chapter {{n-4}}: insert the message into an outbox table in the same transaction as the order, and let a small relay process move outbox rows into the queue. Both the order and its job then exist, or neither does. Two more habits keep jobs and data in step:


    

  • Put ids in jobs, not data. The job carries orderId, and the worker reads the order from the database when it runs. Data copied into the job may be out of date by then.
    • 

  • Keep job data small and free of secrets: it is stored in Redis, visible in dashboards, and kept after the job finishes.
    •

{{n}}.5 Scheduled work



Some jobs are not caused by a request but by the clock. A job scheduler creates a job at every time that matches a cron pattern; here a sales report every morning at six, Cairo time:









The pattern 0 6 * * * reads “minute 0, hour 6, every day of every month, every weekday”. The next run, 04:00 UTC, is six in the morning in Cairo: always give a time zone, or the report moves by an hour twice a year. Because the scheduler lives in Redis, it fires once however many workers are running, which a setInterval in each server could never promise. upsertJobScheduler creates it or updates it, so it can run safely at every start.



{{n}}.6 Running workers in production



Workers are long-running processes, and they need the same care as web servers:






    

  • Graceful shutdown. A deploy stops the old worker with SIGTERM. worker.close() stops taking new jobs and waits for the jobs in hand to finish, so nothing is cut in half. Give it enough time before the platform kills the process.
    • 

  • Separate processes, separate scaling. Web servers scale with traffic, workers with the amount of queued work. Chapter {{n+6}} runs them as separate containers from the same image.
    • 

  • Different queues for different work. A queue of slow PDF exports should not delay password-reset emails. Give urgent work its own queue and its own workers, or at least priorities.
    • 

  • Redis configured for queues. Unlike a cache, a queue must not lose data: persistence on, and maxmemory-policy noeviction, as Chapter {{n-3}} explained.
    • 

  • Visibility. Watch the number of waiting, delayed and failed jobs, and how long jobs wait before they start. A queue that grows all day means the workers cannot keep up.
    • 




Professional habit: design every job to be retried
When you write a job, ask three questions before the code: What happens if it runs twice? What happens if it fails halfway? What happens if it runs an hour late? Write the answers as a comment above the processor, and make the code match them. Jobs that survive these three questions survive production.

Summary



    

  • A request does what its response needs; slow, unreliable or later work becomes a job in a queue.
    • 

  • BullMQ keeps queues in Redis; producers add jobs in a millisecond, workers in separate processes do them.
    • 

  • Attempts with exponential backoff make jobs reliable; UnrecoverableError stops pointless retries; failed jobs wait to be looked at.
    • 

  • Jobs run at least once: make them idempotent, and use job ids so that the same work is queued once.
    • 

  • Queue after the commit or through an outbox; put ids in jobs, not copies of data.
    • 

  • Job schedulers run timed work once across all workers; workers shut down gracefully and are watched like servers.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. queueConfirmation gives every job the id order- plus the order number, and BullMQ does not add a job whose id already exists in the queue, so the second call returned the existing job. 2. After an ordinary error the job is retried, up to attempts, with the backoff between tries; after an UnrecoverableError it goes straight to the failed list, because retrying cannot help. 3. The job may run seconds or hours later, and more than once; reading the order when the job runs gives the current data (it may have been cancelled or changed), and keeps the job small and free of personal details.

Project {{n}}.2 · Medium


Abandoned basket reminders


Remind customers about a full basket 24 hours later, unless they have ordered in the meantime.


You will practise: delayed jobs, cancelling jobs, and checking the current state when a job runs.

Project {{n}}.3 · Challenge


An outbox relay


Guarantee that every committed order gets its confirmation job, even if the process dies right after the commit.


You will practise: the outbox pattern, FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, and at-least-once delivery from end to end.


    

  1. In the order transaction, insert a row into outbox (type, payload, created) instead of queuing.
    2. 

  2. Write a relay that, every second, takes up to 100 unsent rows with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, queues each with the job id taken from the outbox row, and marks them sent.
    3. 

  3. Run two relays at once, and show that each row is queued once.
    4. 

  4. Kill a relay after queuing but before marking rows sent, and show that the job ids stop the duplicates.
    5. 



Hint: the outbox row id makes a perfect job id: if the relay queues the same row twice, BullMQ keeps one job.
