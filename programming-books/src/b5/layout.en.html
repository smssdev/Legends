Layouts That Fit Every Screen

In this chapter you will learn to

  • explain how Flutter decides the size and the place of every widget
  • arrange widgets in rows and columns, and share out the free space
  • recognise an overflow, read its message, and fix it
  • put widgets on top of each other with Stack
  • let items flow onto the next line with Wrap
  • build one screen that fits a small phone, a large phone and a tablet

Chapter {{n-1}} gave you the pieces. This chapter puts them in their places. A real screen has a bar at the top, cards side by side, a price pushed to the far edge, a badge on the corner of an icon, and it must look right on a narrow phone held upright and on a tablet lying on its side. Flutter has a small set of layout widgets that do all of this, and one simple rule behind them. Learn the rule, and layouts stop being guesswork.

When a layout looks wrong, do not change numbers at random. Ask three questions: how much room did this widget get from its parent, how big did it decide to be, and where did its parent put it? This chapter teaches you to answer all three.

{{n}}.1 The rule of layout

Flutter lays out the whole widget tree with one rule, usually summed up in one line:

Constraints go down. Sizes go up. The parent sets the position.

  1. A parent tells each child the constraints it must respect: the smallest and the largest width and height it may have. The screen gives the app its own size; a Padding of 16 gives its child 32 pixels less in each direction; a SizedBox(width: 300) gives exactly 300.
  2. Each child chooses its size within those limits, after asking its own children the same question, and reports it back up.
  3. The parent places each child: Center in the middle, Column one under the other, Row side by side.

A widget does not choose its own position, and cannot be bigger than its constraints allow. Almost every layout problem you will meet is one of these steps doing something you did not expect, and the sections below show the common ones.

{{n}}.2 Rows and columns

A Row places its children side by side, along its main axis, which is horizontal. A Column does the same vertically. The other direction is the cross axis. Two properties decide where the children go: mainAxisAlignment along the main axis, and crossAxisAlignment across it. This app shows the same three coloured boxes in four rows:






start packs the boxes at the beginning, center in the middle, spaceBetween puts all the free space between them, and spaceEvenly gives equal space around each one. In the last row, CrossAxisAlignment.end moves the boxes to the bottom of the 80-pixel band. Box and Labelled are two small widgets written for this example, in the same file; Box is reused all through the chapter.



Tip
“Start” and “end” mean the beginning and the end of the reading direction, not left and right. In an Arabic app a row starts on the right, and every start in your code follows it. Write start and end, never “left” and “right”, and your layouts will work in both directions for free; Chapter 13 uses this.



{{n}}.3 Sharing the space: Expanded and Flexible



Boxes of fixed sizes rarely fill a screen exactly. Expanded and Flexible decide who gets the space that is left:








    

  • In the first row, boxes 1 and 3 keep their 56 pixels, and Expanded makes box 2 take everything else.
    • 

  • In the second, all three are expanded, and flex: 2 gives box 2 two shares of the space for every one share of the others.
    • 

  • In the third, the text is wrapped in Flexible: it may use the space left between the icon and the teal box, and no more, so it goes onto a second line instead of pushing the box off the screen.
    • 



The difference between the two: Expanded must fill its share, while Flexible may use up to its share, and is happy to be smaller.



{{n}}.4 When it does not fit: overflow



What happens without Flexible? A Row gives each child as much width as it wants, and a long text wants to be very wide. The tests show it best, because they report every layout error. Here is a row 300 pixels wide with an icon and a long name:








“A RenderFlex overflowed by 2008 pixels on the right.” A RenderFlex is the object that lays out a Row or a Column, and overflow means its children need more room than it has. The number is so large because tests draw every letter as a square box; in a running app it would be smaller, but the problem would be the same. In a debug build the app shows the overflow as yellow and black stripes along the edge, impossible to miss; a release build hides them, and the text is simply cut off. Professional habit
Test your layouts with the longest real text, not the shortest: the longest dish name on the menu, the user with a double-barrelled surname, the German translation. Text in a row should almost always be inside Expanded or Flexible, and have a plan for overflow (maxLines and overflow, from Chapter {{n-1}}).



{{n}}.5 Layers: Stack



Sometimes widgets must sit on top of each other: a label on a picture, a badge on an icon. Stack paints its children one over the other, the first at the bottom:








A child without position takes the stack’s size: here the brown box sets the size of the banner. Positioned pins a child to edges of the stack: top: 12 is 12 pixels from the top. PositionedDirectional does the same with start and end instead of left and right, so the label moves to the other corner in an Arabic app. Giving both start and end stretches the title row across the banner.



The cart badge sits partly outside the icon, with negative positions. A stack normally clips anything outside its edges; clipBehavior: Clip.none lets the badge show.



{{n}}.6 Flowing onto the next line: Wrap



A Row never goes onto a second line. When you have a number of small items, such as tags or filter chips, use Wrap: it fills a line, then starts the next.








spacing is the gap between chips on a line, and runSpacing the gap between lines. On a wider screen the same code puts more chips on each line, without any change.

{{n}}.7 Every screen size



Your app will run on phones of many widths, held upright or sideways, and on tablets. Two tools tell you how much room you have:


    

  • MediaQuery.sizeOf(context) gives the size of the whole screen.
    • 

  • LayoutBuilder gives the constraints of the place where it sits, and lets you build different widgets for different widths. Prefer it: a widget should depend on the room it gets, not on the size of the screen, so that it also works inside half of a tablet.
    • 











The first line of the app prints what MediaQuery says. Below it, LayoutBuilder chooses: under 600 pixels, a column; from 600, a row with Expanded panels. The number 600 is a breakpoint, the width where the layout changes. Material’s guidelines use 600 as the boundary between phones and tablets, which is why so many apps use it too.



Watch out
Real phones have notches, rounded corners and a home bar that cover parts of the screen. Wrap the content of a screen in SafeArea when it has no AppBar, and Flutter keeps it clear of them. The screenshots in this book come from a browser, which has none of these, but your phone does.

Summary



    

  • Constraints go down, sizes go up, the parent sets the position.
    • 

  • Row and Column place children along the main axis; mainAxisAlignment and crossAxisAlignment place them along and across it.
    • 

  • Expanded fills the free space and flex shares it; Flexible may use up to its share.
    • 

  • An overflow means children need more room than they got; read the message, and give text Flexible room.
    • 

  • Stack layers widgets; Positioned and PositionedDirectional pin them to edges; Clip.none lets them stick out.
    • 

  • Wrap flows items onto more lines.
    • 

  • LayoutBuilder builds different layouts for different widths; 600 pixels is the usual breakpoint between phone and tablet. SafeArea avoids notches.
    • 




Quick check answers: 1. mainAxisAlignment: a column’s main axis is vertical. 2. Expanded must fill its share of the free space; Flexible may take up to its share, but can be smaller. 3. Because start follows the reading direction: the widget moves to the right side in an Arabic app without any change to the code.
