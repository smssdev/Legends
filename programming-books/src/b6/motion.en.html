Chapter {{n}}

Animations That Feel Native

In this chapter you will learn to

  • turn motion into tokens: a few durations and a few curves, chosen once
  • read a curve as numbers, and choose one for things that arrive, leave and move
  • animate by changing a value, with implicit widgets, and drive a motion yourself with a controller
  • fly a widget between two pages with a Hero, and stagger a list with one controller
  • respect people who ask for less motion, and prove it with a test

Motion is how an app explains itself. A card that grows says “something was added here”; a picture that flies from a list to a page says “this is the same drink, closer”. Done well, nobody notices it. Done badly, it is the first thing people notice: a jump, a stutter, a fade that takes too long when you are in a hurry. This chapter treats motion like Chapter {{n-1}} treated colour: a few named decisions, components that use them, and tests that check them.

Every number in this chapter was printed by a test, frame by frame, and the filmstrips are real screenshots taken while the motion ran. To make the browser fast enough to catch them, those apps run ten times slower; section {{n}}.5 shows the one line that does it.

{{n}}.1 Motion tokens

An animation needs two decisions: how long, and how the speed changes along the way, the curve. Both become tokens:




    

  • Three durations are enough for most apps: short for small things (a badge, a switch), medium for a card or a page, long for large movements across the screen.
    • 

  • The curves come from Material 3’s Easing. Things that arrive start fast and settle gently, so they appear at once and land softly; things that leave start slowly and speed away; things that move on screen use the standard curve.
    • 

  • motion(context, duration) returns zero when the person has asked the system for less motion. Every animation in the app asks for its duration through it.
    • 




A curve maps the time that has passed, from 0 to 1, to how far the animation has gone. A test prints four of them:






A quarter of the way through the time, linear has done a quarter of the movement and easeInOut only 13%; Material’s emphasizedDecelerate has done 83%. That is why arrivals with it feel quick even at 300 ms: almost everything happens in the first quarter, and the rest is the landing. A linear animation feels mechanical for the opposite reason: nothing in the real world starts and stops at full speed.



{{n}}.2 Implicit animations



The easiest animation in Flutter is not to write one. Widgets whose names start with Animated watch their own values; when a rebuild gives them a new value, they move to it by themselves. The order card grows, changes colour and rounds its corners when the first latte is added, and the total fades in:






The screen only calls setState(() => count++). A test taps the button and prints the total’s opacity and the card’s height every 50 ms:






The two columns follow their curves: after 50 ms the total is already at 74% opacity (an arrival, with Motion.enter), while the height has done 40% of its way (Motion.move). And here is the same moment in the browser, slowed down:





The badge uses AnimatedSwitcher, which animates between two different children. It knows they are different by their keys: ValueKey(count) makes the badge for 1 a new widget, not the badge for 0 with new text. Without the key, Flutter would update the text in place, and nothing would move. Project {{n}}.1 proves that both numbers are on screen while they cross.



Watch out
The first version of this card was a Column with the total under a Spacer. At 96 pixels high there was no room for the invisible total, and the test failed with “A RenderFlex overflowed by 12 pixels on the bottom”: an invisible widget still takes its space. The card now places the total with a Stack and clips its children (clipBehavior: Clip.hardEdge), so the total is revealed as the card grows instead of pushing against it.



{{n}}.3 Explicit animations



Implicit widgets animate from one value to another. Some motion has no end value: a ring that turns while the coffee is brewing, a pulse on a live order. For those you hold the clock yourself, an AnimationController:






    

  • A controller needs a vsync, a ticker that calls it once per frame. SingleTickerProviderStateMixin gives the state one, and stops it when the page is not visible, so a hidden ring costs nothing.
    • 

  • ..repeat() runs it from 0 to 1 every two seconds, for ever, and dispose stops it. Forgetting dispose leaves a ticker running after the page has closed; in debug builds Flutter stops with an error that names the state with the active ticker.
    • 

  • AnimatedBuilder rebuilds its builder on every frame. Anything that does not move goes in child: it is built once and handed to the builder each time.
    • 










The cup counts its own builds, and a test runs 60 frames:






One build for 60 frames. Had the cup been written inside the builder, it would have been built 61 times: harmless for one icon, expensive for a card full of text and pictures. Chapter {{n+1}} measures what that costs.

{{n}}.4 Heroes



When a person opens a drink, its picture should travel from the list to the page, so the eye follows it. Flutter calls this a Hero: give the same tag to a widget on each page, and during the navigation Flutter lifts it out of the first page and flies it to its place on the second.










Nothing else was needed: no controller, no positions. Two rules keep heroes working. A tag must be unique on a page (two heroes with the tag latte on one list is an error), so tags come from ids, not names. And the two widgets should look alike at both ends, because Flutter flies the destination’s widget, resized; a circle that becomes a square mid-flight looks like a glitch.



{{n}}.5 Slowing time



The filmstrips in this chapter were taken from versions of the apps that start with one extra line:






timeDilation stretches every animation in the app. It is for people, not for the app: set it to 5 or 10 while you work on a transition, and you see what happens in each frame instead of a blur. It is also how a design review should look at motion. Never ship it.



{{n}}.6 Less motion



For some people, movement on screen causes dizziness or nausea; others simply prefer a calmer phone. Both Android (“Remove animations”) and iOS (“Reduce Motion”) have a setting, and Flutter reports it as MediaQuery.disableAnimationsOf(context). Because every animation here takes its duration from motion(), one test checks the whole card:








One frame after the tap, the card is already at its final height. Less motion does not mean no feedback: the card still grows and the total still appears, just without the journey. Animations that carry meaning (a progress ring, a loading indicator) should keep moving; decoration should stop.



Professional habit
Review motion like you review layout: with the slowed-down build, on a real mid-range phone, and once with “Remove animations” turned on. Most motion bugs are visible only in one of those three: a jump that a fast transition hides, a stutter that a fast laptop hides, and a screen that waits for an animation that never comes.

Summary



    

  • Motion has tokens too: a few durations and three curves, for arriving, leaving and moving.
    • 

  • A curve maps time to progress; arrivals do most of their movement early and land softly.
    • 

  • Implicit Animated… widgets move to new values by themselves; AnimatedSwitcher needs a key to see a new child.
    • 

  • An AnimationController drives motion that has no end value; static parts go in AnimatedBuilder’s child.
    • 

  • A Hero flies a widget between pages by its tag; timeDilation slows everything down for review.
    • 

  • Every duration passes through motion(), so reduced motion is one test.
    •

So that what arrives is visible at once and then settles gently: most of the movement happens in the first quarter of the time (83% with emphasizedDecelerate), which feels quick, and the slow end feels like a landing rather than a stop. 2. Every part of the picture that does not change from frame to frame; it is built once and passed to the builder on each frame.
